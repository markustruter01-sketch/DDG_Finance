Three accounts, one year

1. The question

Can a rules-based system, run without a human touching it, grow money more reliably than simply owning the S&P 500? Not "beat it every year": over forty years almost nothing beats a buy-and-hold index on raw return without taking more risk. The useful question is narrower: can we earn a return close to the index's while losing far less in the bad years, so that the money can be left alone through a crash?

To answer it we run three genuinely different strategies on three separate $100,000 paper accounts at the same broker for the calendar year 2027, measure them on identical days against each other and against the S&P 500, and decide at the end which, if any, deserves real money. One year on paper is a thin test; it is also the only honest one, because every strategy here was designed by someone who has already seen the history.

AccountStrategyThe bet, in one line
AtlasFLEET-V1-F1Index-like return, half the drawdown: a geared equity trend core plus a diversifying sleeve of bonds, gold, commodities and the dollar.
BullionFLEET-V1-C1Atlas's equity core on its own. Answers whether the diversifying sleeve earns its 35%.
MomentumMOMO-V2Fifty S&P 500 stocks by momentum, with the brakes applied to the stock book itself. An experiment with no history behind it.

2. How we keep ourselves honest

Most backtests are fiction, because the person running them adjusts the rules until the past looks good. The platform is built so that this is hard to do by accident and visible when done on purpose.

  • Plans before runs. Every strategy has a written plan, committed to the code repository before any data is touched: the exact rules, the candidate variants, the controls, the costs, the time windows, the selection rule and the pass bar. Nothing in the plan can be changed after a result is seen; amendments are appended, dated and explained.
  • Locked history. Data from the held-out years is stored in a separate, locked part of each dataset. Code cannot read it without a registration that names the dataset, and a registration can be used for one test, once. A second attempt is refused by the software even if the first one crashed.
  • Counted attempts. Every strategy tried on this platform is counted (22 so far). The count feeds a deflated Sharpe ratio, which asks: given how many things we tried, how likely is this result to be luck?
  • Controls, always. Every run reports the plain index (B), and usually a "null" that does the same trading without the signal, so a strategy must beat not only the market but also its own noise.
  • A fail is a fail. Two of the four strategies we tried failed their own declared bar and were written up as failures (section 6). The failures are in the same log as the successes.
  • Server numbers only. The dashboard formats figures computed on the server from the broker's own records; the browser never derives a return.

3. Atlas: FLEET-V1-F1

The rules

Two sleeves, rebalanced monthly, 65% and 35% of the account.

  • Equity core (65%). Each month the S&P 500's trend is read over three horizons (3, 6 and 12 months). Each horizon that is rising casts a vote. Three votes in a calm market means up to 1.5× exposure using leveraged ETFs; one vote means half exposure; none means cash. Exposure is scaled down when 20-day volatility rises above 15%. The core splits 60/40 between S&P and Nasdaq-100 instruments. Every position has a 20% stop.
  • Cross-asset sleeve (35%). Long-only trend in four lines that have historically done well when stocks did badly: 10-year Treasuries, gold, commodities and the US dollar. Each line is held when it is in an uptrend, otherwise its share sits in T-bills.
  • Cash earns the T-bill rate through a short-duration Treasury ETF (SGOV).

Why these rules

Time-series momentum, "hold what has been rising, step aside when it stops", is the most replicated result in empirical finance, documented across every major asset class and a century of data (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen 2012; Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen 2017). Its value is not a higher average return but a different shape: it cuts the worst falls roughly in half while keeping most of the upside, because a crash takes time and a trend rule leaves during it. Combining an equity trend sleeve with trend in bonds, gold, commodities and currencies is the structure of the managed-futures industry, which has forty years of live track records. The 65/35 split, the vote counts and the 15% volatility line were fixed in the plan before the first run and were not tuned afterwards.

4. Bullion: FLEET-V1-C1

C1 is F1's equity core run on its own, 100% of the account, same votes, same volatility scaling, same stops, no cross-asset sleeve. It was explored alongside F1 in the same run and simulated in the same held-out test as one of F1's components. It is on Bullion's account because the third strategy we planned for it failed (section 6), and because the comparison between F1 and C1 is the most useful one the platform can run: it isolates the one design choice in F1 that costs return in good years, the 35% held in bonds, gold, commodities and the dollar.

C1 earns more than F1 and falls further: the sleeve it drops is what buys F1's shallower drawdowns. In 2021–26 C1 beat the index on return. Whether that holds in a year with a real crash is exactly what 2027 may show.

5. Momentum: MOMO-V2

The rules

  • Universe: the S&P 500's current members, read from the index fund's published holdings on each signal day.
  • Ranking: 12-1 momentum (the return from twelve months ago to one month ago). The top 50 are held at 2% each.
  • Brakes on the book, not the market: an equal-weight index of the 50 names themselves is computed each day. The book is held only while that index is above its 100-day average; otherwise everything goes to cash until the next monthly pick. The invested fraction is scaled so the book's volatility targets 15%. Every name has a 20% stop; a stopped name's slot waits in cash until the next month.
  • Rebalance monthly. Between months only the brake can trade.

Why it is an experiment

Cross-sectional stock momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993) is the second most documented premium in finance, and the most dangerous: it suffers "momentum crashes" in sharp rebounds, when the stocks that fell most bounce hardest (Daniel and Moskowitz 2016). Our first attempt, MOMO-V1, failed for two reasons that MOMO-V2 is designed around: it picked from the most-traded stocks, which filled the book with speculative small caps, and it used the S&P 500's trend as its brake, which cannot see a momentum crash happening inside a healthy index. MOMO-V2 picks only from index members and applies the brakes to the book's own index. These are documented fixes, not tuned parameters, and they have no backtest behind them on this platform: the owner chose to trade individual stocks, and this is the most defensible way to do it. It is run as a forward test. If it passes its 2027 bar it earns a second year and a registration; it is not a candidate for real money on one year's evidence.

Bullion was planned for this strategy and runs C1 instead.

7. Safety: the rules that cannot be argued with

Every strategy here will have a bad month. The platform's job is to make sure a bad month cannot become a ruinous one, and that no person has to be awake for that to hold.

  • Per-account drawdown governor. Each account's equity is marked daily against its high-water mark. More than 15% below it: every target is halved until equity recovers to 95% of the mark. More than 22% below: the account is sold to cash and halts; only the owner, with a confirmation token, can restart it.
  • Fleet kill switch. A separate process checks every five minutes that the broker's records and the platform's ledger agree, that every strategy position has its protective stop at the broker, and that the fleet as a whole is not past its drawdown line. Any failure pauses every account and preserves the stops. It is armed by default and proved itself on day one, pausing the fleet on a bookkeeping race in the vault's first sale (fixed the same morning). A system that halts on a false alarm is preferable to one that does not halt on a true one.
  • Stops at the broker. Every stock and leveraged-ETF position carries a 20% stop order held by the broker, not by our software, so it survives our outage. The four cross-asset lines in Atlas are exempt by declared rule (their exit is the trend), and the kill switch knows that.
  • No borrowing, whole shares, cash in T-bills. Exposure above 1× comes only from leveraged ETFs, never margin; the number of shares is always covered by cash; idle cash earns the bill rate.
  • Hourly cycles, not continuous trading. Each account acts once an hour during the US session from completed daily bars. There is nothing to overtrade with.

8. The vault

Trading capital is expected to compound at 10–15% a year in good times and to spend months underwater in bad ones. The vault is a second, slower balance that only ever grows: on the first session of each month, if an account's trading equity is above its previous high-water mark, 10% of the gain above the mark is moved into 1–3 month Treasury bills (BIL) inside the same account, and the mark resets to the new, post-skim equity. In a down month, or a month that is recovering towards an old high, nothing moves. The vault is never traded, never sold except by the owner's explicit action, and is excluded from every strategy's capital and from the stop checks.

Ten percent because the arithmetic has a sweet spot: it costs the bots about one to one and a half points of compounding a year and builds a meaningful second income within a few years; 20% starves the engine; 5% does not matter for a decade. Net new highs because that is the only honest definition of profit: skimming individual winning trades while the account is falling moves money out of a losing account and punishes strategies that trade often. Account performance is reported on total equity including the vault, so skimming never makes a strategy look worse than it is.

9. The 2027 verdict

All three accounts are measured on the same trading days from 1 January to 31 December 2027, on total equity including the vault, after all costs. Each has a pass bar declared in its plan; the bars are against the S&P 500 over the same days, not against each other.

AccountPass bar (all must hold)
Atlas · F1Sharpe ≥ S&P's + 0.10 · worst fall no worse than −25% and shallower than the S&P's · return ≥ S&P's · deflated Sharpe > 0.95
Bullion · C1Judged by the same bar as F1, and against F1: it must beat F1 on return by more than it loses to F1 on worst fall, or the sleeve has earned its place.
Momentum · MOMO-V2Return ≥ S&P's + 2 points · worst fall no worse than −25% and no worse than 0.75× the S&P's · Sharpe ≥ S&P's. A pass earns a second paper year, not real money.

One year will not settle a question that took forty years of data to frame. What it can do is catch a strategy behaving unlike its history (a drawdown deeper than any it has shown, a correlation it should not have), and it can show the owner, live and with money that is only pretend, what each of these feels like to hold. Real money follows the strategy whose live year matched its evidence, not the one that happened to earn most.

10. What we expect, and why Atlas is the best-founded bet

The honest prediction is a shape, not a ranking.

  • In a straight-up year (2017, 2019, 2021, 2024) the order will most likely be Momentum or Bullion, then Atlas, then possibly the S&P between them. Atlas will lag by a few points: a third of it is in assets that do not rise with stocks.
  • In a year with a real bear market (2008, 2022) Atlas should lose roughly half of what the index loses, Bullion somewhat more than Atlas, and Momentum is the unknown: its brakes are designed for exactly this and have never been tested live.
  • In a sharp V-shaped crash and rebound (2020) all three will lag the index on the way back up, Momentum most, and the year will probably favour the S&P.

Why, then, do we say Atlas is the best-founded of the three, rather than the one most likely to top the table? Because "best" for real money means the strategy whose behaviour we understand well enough to keep holding when it is losing, and on that measure the gap is wide:

  1. Forty years of evidence, in two independent halves. F1 was selected on 1987–2010 and then tested, once, on 2011–2020 it had never seen. It missed the return bar in the calmest decade on record and kept every promise about risk: worst fall half the index's in both halves, Sharpe above the index's in exploration and within 0.03 of it in the held-out decade, deflated Sharpe 0.98. No other strategy here has a held-out test it passed on risk.
  2. The failure mode is known and bounded. Trend following loses by whipsaw: it steps aside, the market resumes, it re-enters higher. That costs a few points in a choppy year and never produces a catastrophe. Momentum's failure mode is a crash concentrated in the most crowded names; put-writing's was a gap down with insurance sold. We chose the strategy whose worst case is boredom.
  3. It is diversified by construction, not by hope. F1's correlation to the S&P over 2011–2020 was 0.73, and the cross-asset sleeve's was −0.14. When stocks fall, part of the book is designed to be somewhere else. C1 and MOMO-V2 are both 100% equity exposure when fully invested.
  4. It survives being run by nobody. The rules fit on one page, read daily bars, and trade once a month; every live cycle is reconciled against the broker within the hour. The person who will have to leave it alone during a drawdown can understand why it is doing what it is doing.

Bullion exists to test the one thing that could overturn that judgment: if C1 beats F1 by more than the sleeve's drawdown protection is worth, over a year that includes a real fall, then the sleeve is dead weight and the real-money candidate is C1. Momentum exists because the owner wants to trade stocks and this is the most defensible way to find out whether that is a good idea.

11. What this paper does not claim

  • It does not claim that any of these will beat the S&P 500 in 2027. Over 24 years of exploration F1 did; over the next 10 it did not; over the 6 after that it did not on return and did on risk.
  • It does not claim the backtests are the truth. The pre-1993 instruments are rebuilt from indices with declared haircuts; costs are assumed, not observed; every designer had seen the history. The live year is the test precisely because the history cannot be.
  • It does not claim one year is enough. A pass earns continued trust and, for Atlas or Bullion, a real-money allocation sized for the drawdown the history showed, not a larger one. A fail is a fail, and will be written up as the two above were.
  • It does not claim the platform is finished. It claims the platform is honest: every number on the dashboard can be traced to a broker record, every rule to a dated plan, and every failure is in the same log as every success.
